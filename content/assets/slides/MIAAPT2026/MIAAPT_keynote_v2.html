Speaker Notes: [~2 min · end by 6:30] Before the framework, a picture my colleague Dirk Colbry likes to draw. Theory and experiment are usually described as the two pillars that hold up the house of science. Over the last couple of decades, many people have argued that computation, simulation, and data are a third pillar. I don't think that's quite right. Computation isn't a separate pillar off to the side. It's the foundation underneath both. Computers are how we develop theories and how we make observations, and even physicists who would never call themselves computational use them every day. That framing matters for teaching. If computation is a third pillar, it's natural to teach it as a separate skill that ends in a separate product: code. If it's the foundation, then computing is part of how we do physics, and what we should care about is the thinking it supports. That's where epistemic programming comes in.

Speaker Notes: [~2 min · end by 8:30] I'm borrowing the term "epistemic programming" from an ITiCSE working group I served on this past summer, with thirteen researchers from Europe and the US. The core idea: in standard programming you meet a specification and deliver a pre-defined product. In epistemic programming the computer is a partner in figuring something out, and the outcome isn't known in advance. We ran a Delphi-style consensus process. The one property every voter agreed was defining, 11 of 11, was that the goal is to construct knowledge. The others: it starts from an inquiry question, it moves forward as insights arrive, it is co-constructed with the computer, and it produces a knowledge artifact. Physics teachers who model with VPython, Glowscript, or spreadsheets are already doing this. The framework gives it a name and a structure. And I want to be clear about the order here. We didn't design CMSE 201 from this framework. We built it in 2015, and the working group named epistemic programming in 2026, partly by studying work like our students'. That's the story of this talk: the practice came first, and the name came later. EXPAND: Tell the Delphi story. It started with 14 candidate properties, and at the conference we voted with stickers: green for defining, blue for relevant, red for neither. Surprise: 'tinkering, trial-and-error, and productive failure' was judged relevant but NOT defining (9 of 13 votes). EP is about where the process is headed, not just messing around. Also point out that the same practice goes by other names, like computational modeling, exploratory programming, and simulation. Many people in the room already do this.

Speaker Notes: [~3.5 min · end by 12:00] This is the working group's interaction-based framework, Figure 4 straight from our report. There are four stages: the epistemic interest, which is the question; the programmed artifact, which is the code or model; the program output; and the insights. Between them are transitional phases. Notice there are two arrows from the question to the code: students design an investigation and implement it, and they go back and forth between those two a lot. Then they execute the program, make sense of the evidence, and then reframe the question, which starts the loop again. The point for today is that each arrow is an action a student takes, and actions can be taught and assessed. We tested the framework on 60 student computational essays from two places: a vocational high school in Switzerland and CMSE 201 here at MSU, from Fall 2019. So this isn't abstract; it describes our own students. We saw a tight inner loop between designing and coding, a wider outer loop through evidence sensemaking, and very little documented inquiry framing, where students revisit their own question. I'll come back to that when we get to assessment. EXPAND: Walk the cycle with a physics example the audience knows, such as projectile motion with drag. Question: how much does air resistance matter for a baseball? Design a model, code it, run it, read the plot, then reframe: 'so what launch angle is best now?' Speaker Notes: [~2 min · end by 39:00] You don't need a new department to do this. Most teachers we interviewed had never heard the term epistemic programming but recognized it right away in what they already do with simulations and data analysis. So treat this as a lens, not a new paradigm. To start small: begin an assignment with a question instead of a set of instructions, have students write their reasoning next to their code, add one reflection prompt, and grade early attempts on effort so failure is safe. Over time, add scaffolds such as templates, partial programs, or block-based tools for younger students, and shift your role toward coaching.
